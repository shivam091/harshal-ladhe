How a Lighthouse Throws Its Light

Early lighthouses burned wood or coal in an open brazier, and most of the light escaped upwards into the sky, where no ship could use it.

In 1822 the French physicist Augustin Fresnel presented a lens that changed that. A Fresnel lens is built from concentric rings of glass prisms, each angled to bend stray light into one flat, horizontal beam.

Because the rings replace one thick block of glass, the lens is far lighter than a solid lens of the same power, and a single lamp behind it can be seen from more than twenty nautical miles away.

Telling One Light from Another

Every light along a coast has its own characteristic: a pattern of flashes, occultations and colours printed on nautical charts, so a navigator can identify a lighthouse by timing it.

  • A fixed light shines continuously.
  • A flashing light is dark for longer than it is lit.
  • An occulting light is lit for longer than it is dark.

Today most lighthouses are automated, and many Fresnel lenses have been replaced by LED beacons, but the old lenses still sit in museums where visitors can watch them throw light across the room.
