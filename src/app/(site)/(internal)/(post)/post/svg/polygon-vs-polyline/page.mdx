## Introduction

In [SVG Essentials: Mastering Shapes, Coordinates, and Styling](/post/svg/svg-essentials-mastering-basic-shapes), we covered the six core SVG shapes and the coordinate system they all share.
Two of those elements, `<polygon>` and `<polyline>`, deserve a closer look on their own: they take an identical `points` syntax and render it in two completely different ways.

This post starts with a short recap and then goes well past the basics:

- how fill rules actually decide what's "inside", with a ray-crossing walkthrough;
- why a polyline that repeats its first point still isn't a polygon;
- how `stroke-miterlimit` quietly bevels sharp star tips;
- generating points with JavaScript, drawing sparklines, and putting markers on vertices;
- animating a line drawing itself in, and morphing one shape into another;
- the performance, accessibility and "time to switch to `<path>`" decisions that come up in real charts, icons and route maps.

## Polygon vs Polyline at a Glance

| Feature          | `<polyline>`                                              | `<polygon>`                                  |
| ---------------- | --------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------- |
| Shape type       | Open                                                      | Closed                                       |
| Final segment    | Not drawn; the stroke stops at the last point             | Drawn automatically back to the first point  |
| Fill behavior    | Still filled by default (`fill` is black), so usually set `fill="none"` | Filled by default, which is usually what you want |
| `stroke-linecap` | Visible at both loose ends                                | No loose ends (unless the stroke is dashed)  |
| Best use cases   | Charts, sparklines, waveforms, routes                     | Geometric shapes, UI icons, stars, badges    |

Neither element can draw a curve. For curves, arcs or holes, you need `<path>`; see [When to Switch to `<path>`](#when-to-switch-to-path) below.

**Example Comparison**

The two shapes below use the **exact same `points` string**. Only the element differs, and that one swap is the whole difference between an open line and a closed, filled shape.

<SvgSnippet>
{`
  <svg viewBox="0 0 220 110" width="440" height="220">
    <g>
      <polyline points="10,80 30,20 50,60 70,20 90,80" fill="none" stroke="#3b82f6" stroke-width="2" stroke-linecap="round" stroke-linejoin="round" />
      <text x="50" y="105" font-size="8" text-anchor="middle" fill="currentColor">polyline (open)</text>
    </g>
    <g transform="translate(120,0)">
      <polygon points="10,80 30,20 50,60 70,20 90,80" fill="#3b82f6" fill-opacity="0.3" stroke="#3b82f6" stroke-width="2" stroke-linejoin="round" />
      <text x="50" y="105" font-size="8" text-anchor="middle" fill="currentColor">polygon (closed)</text>
    </g>
  </svg>
`}
</SvgSnippet>

Under the hood, both elements work out their **fill** the same way. The browser treats the shape as closed, with an imaginary segment from the last point back to the first. What differs is the **stroke**: `<polygon>` actually strokes that closing segment and joins it neatly to the first one, while `<polyline>` leaves it unstroked. That's why a polyline without `fill="none"` still paints a solid region:

## Quick Recap: `points`, `<polyline>`, and `<polygon>`

If you've read [the basic shapes post](/post/svg/svg-essentials-mastering-basic-shapes#the-points-list-syntax), this section is a refresher. Otherwise, it's everything you need for the rest of this post.

**The `points` attribute.** Both elements take a list of X/Y pairs in user units (no `px`). Each pair is one vertex, and the vertices are connected in the order they're written. Commas and whitespace are interchangeable separators, so these three are equivalent. The first is the most readable:

<CodeBlock language="xml">
{`
  <polyline points="10,10 50,60 90,20" />  <!-- recommended: comma inside a pair, space between pairs -->
  <polyline points="10 10 50 60 90 20" />
  <polyline points="10 10, 50 60, 90 20" />
`}
</CodeBlock>

**Malformed lists.** An **odd** number of values leaves a dangling coordinate with no partner. The spec treats that as an error. In practice, engines usually render the complete pairs and ignore the leftover number, but error handling has changed between spec versions, so treat an odd count as a bug to fix rather than behaviour to rely on. An **empty** list, or one with a single point, has no segment to draw and renders nothing.

**`<polyline>`** connects the points and stops at the last one. Because `fill` defaults to black, always add `fill="none"` unless you really want the region shaded:

<CodeBlock language="xml">
{`
  <polyline points="10,80 40,20 70,60 100,30" stroke="blue" />              <!-- phantom black fill -->
  <polyline points="10,80 40,20 70,60 100,30" stroke="blue" fill="none" />  <!-- just the line -->
`}
</CodeBlock>

**`<polygon>`** connects the points and then seals the shape with a segment from the last point back to the first:

<CodeBlock language="xml">
{`
  <polygon points="50,10 90,80 10,80" fill="lightblue" stroke="navy" stroke-width="2" />
`}
</CodeBlock>

<Info title="Not to be confused with CSS clip-path: polygon()">
  CSS has its own, unrelated `polygon()` function used inside `clip-path` (e.g. `clip-path: polygon(50% 0%, 100% 100%, 0% 100%)`). It shares the name and the "list of points" idea, but it clips the visible region of *any* HTML or SVG element. It isn't an SVG element, and it has nothing to do with the `<polygon>` tag covered in this post.
</Info>

## Explicit Closing: A Repeated Point Isn't a Polygon

A common shortcut is to "close" a polyline by repeating its first point at the end. The outline *looks* closed, but it isn't the same as a `<polygon>`. The seam where the two ends meet is drawn as **two separate line ends**, not as a corner, so `stroke-linecap` applies there and `stroke-linejoin` doesn't.

With the default `butt` caps and a thick stroke, that shows up as a notch at the starting corner:

<SvgSnippet>
{`
  <svg viewBox="0 0 220 110" width="440" height="220">
    <g>
      <polyline points="20,20 80,20 80,80 20,80 20,20" fill="none" stroke="#f43f5e" stroke-width="12" />
      <text x="50" y="105" font-size="8" text-anchor="middle" fill="currentColor">polyline, first point repeated</text>
    </g>
    <g transform="translate(110,0)">
      <polygon points="20,20 80,20 80,80 20,80" fill="none" stroke="#10b981" stroke-width="12" />
      <text x="50" y="105" font-size="8" text-anchor="middle" fill="currentColor">polygon</text>
    </g>
  </svg>
`}
</SvgSnippet>

Look at the top-left corner of the red square. The first segment's butt cap and the last segment's butt cap both stop flush at `20,20`, so the outer corner square is never painted. The polygon draws a real miter join there, just like its other three corners. `stroke-linecap="square"` happens to fill the gap at a 90° corner, but not at any other angle, so it's a coincidence rather than a fix.

The seam affects more than caps:

- **Dashes**: on the polyline, a dash that would continue across the seam is cut into two capped ends instead of turning the corner with a join. In *both* versions the dash pattern starts at the first point, so the seam can show a partial dash. You can hide it by making the total length a whole number of pattern repeats, which `pathLength` makes easy.
- **Markers**: in both versions, `marker-start` and `marker-end` land on the same spot, the first point. The difference is orientation: with `orient="auto"`, a polygon's start and end markers point along the bisector of the closing and first segments, while the polyline's start marker follows only the first segment and its end marker only the last.
- **Length**: both versions include the closing segment in `getTotalLength()` and `pathLength`, so a draw-on animation behaves the same either way.

<Success title="Rule of thumb">
  If the outline should be closed, use `<polygon>` (or a `<path>` ending in `Z`). Only repeat the first point on a polyline when you need its caps, for example a closed loop with deliberately rounded ends at the seam.
</Success>

## Point Order: Why Sequence is Strategy

Points are drawn in the **exact sequence** they appear in the string. Changing the order changes the shape's topology.

For a triangle, the order doesn't change the silhouette. Once there are 4 or more points, it does. Scramble the order and a clean square becomes a self-intersecting "hourglass" or "bow-tie":

<SvgSnippet>
{`
  <svg viewBox="0 0 220 125" width="440" height="250">
    <g>
      <polygon points="15,15 85,15 85,85 15,85" fill="#6366f1" fill-opacity="0.25" stroke="#4338ca" stroke-width="2" />
      <text x="8" y="12" font-size="8" text-anchor="middle" fill="currentColor">1</text>
      <text x="92" y="12" font-size="8" text-anchor="middle" fill="currentColor">2</text>
      <text x="92" y="96" font-size="8" text-anchor="middle" fill="currentColor">3</text>
      <text x="8" y="96" font-size="8" text-anchor="middle" fill="currentColor">4</text>
      <text x="50" y="118" font-size="8" text-anchor="middle" fill="currentColor">15,15 85,15 85,85 15,85</text>
    </g>
    <g transform="translate(120,0)">
      <polygon points="15,15 85,85 85,15 15,85" fill="#f43f5e" fill-opacity="0.25" stroke="#be123c" stroke-width="2" />
      <text x="8" y="12" font-size="8" text-anchor="middle" fill="currentColor">1</text>
      <text x="92" y="96" font-size="8" text-anchor="middle" fill="currentColor">2</text>
      <text x="92" y="12" font-size="8" text-anchor="middle" fill="currentColor">3</text>
      <text x="8" y="96" font-size="8" text-anchor="middle" fill="currentColor">4</text>
      <text x="50" y="118" font-size="8" text-anchor="middle" fill="currentColor">15,15 85,85 85,15 15,85</text>
    </g>
  </svg>
`}
</SvgSnippet>

Incorrect ordering can cause:

- Self-intersections, where edges cross each other
- Unexpected fills, because overlapping regions are now decided by the [fill rule](#fill-rules-managing-overlap)
- Visual artifacts, such as spiky miter joins at the new, very sharp corners

When you generate points from data or geometry, sort them deliberately: by angle around a centre for radial shapes, or by x for time series.

## Fill Rules: Managing Overlap

When a shape overlaps itself, like a five-pointed star or a figure-eight, SVG needs a rule to decide which regions are "inside" (filled) and which are "outside" (transparent). That rule is the `fill-rule` property, and both of its values use the same test:

1. Pick a point you want to classify.
2. Draw a ray from that point out to infinity, in any direction.
3. Look at every edge of the shape that crosses the ray.

The two rules differ only in how they score those crossings.

### `nonzero` (default)

`nonzero` cares about the **direction** each edge was drawn in, which comes from the order of your `points`. Start a counter at zero. Each time an edge crosses the ray from left to right (as seen looking along the ray), add 1. Each time one crosses from right to left, subtract 1. The final count is the point's **winding number**. If it's anything other than zero, the point is inside.

The sign of the result doesn't matter, only whether it's zero. Reverse every point and each +1 becomes −1, so a winding number of 2 becomes −2. That's still non-zero, and the fill doesn't change.

### `evenodd`

`evenodd` ignores direction completely and just **counts** crossings. An odd count means inside and the point is filled. An even count means outside and it's left empty.

### Walking Through the Star

Here's the same test applied to two points in a star drawn as a single `<polygon>`. Each small arrowhead shows the direction that edge was drawn in:

<FillRuleRayDiagram />

- **Point A**, in the central pentagon: its ray crosses two edges, and both run the same way. Under `nonzero` that's +1 +1 = 2, so the centre is filled. Under `evenodd` it's 2 crossings, an even number, so the centre is empty.
- **Point B**, in one of the arms: its ray crosses a single edge. That's 1 under `nonzero` and odd under `evenodd`, so both rules fill the arms.

That's the whole difference. The rules only disagree about regions whose winding number is **even but not zero** (2, −2, 4…). An arm is enclosed once, so both fill it; the centre pentagon is enclosed twice in the same direction, so only `nonzero` does.

### Side-by-Side Comparison

The same `points` string rendered with each rule:

<SvgSnippet>
{`
  <svg viewBox="0 0 220 110" width="440" height="220">
    <g>
      <polygon points="50,5 76.4,86.4 7.2,36.1 92.8,36.1 23.5,86.4" fill="#8b5cf6" fill-opacity="0.6" stroke="#6d28d9" stroke-width="2" fill-rule="nonzero" />
      <text x="50" y="105" font-size="9" text-anchor="middle" fill="currentColor">fill-rule: nonzero</text>
    </g>
    <g transform="translate(120,0)">
      <polygon points="50,5 76.4,86.4 7.2,36.1 92.8,36.1 23.5,86.4" fill="#8b5cf6" fill-opacity="0.6" stroke="#6d28d9" stroke-width="2" fill-rule="evenodd" />
      <text x="50" y="105" font-size="9" text-anchor="middle" fill="currentColor">fill-rule: evenodd</text>
    </g>
  </svg>
`}
</SvgSnippet>

The stroke is identical in both, because `fill-rule` only affects the fill.

### What a Single `<polygon>` Can't Do: Holes

It's tempting to reach for `evenodd` to punch a hole in a shape, like a donut, a picture frame or the letter "O". **A `<polygon>` can't do that.** It's always exactly one closed outline (one *subpath*). With one outline, the only regions a fill rule can ever hollow out are the ones created where the outline **crosses itself**, as in the star.

A real hole needs a second outline, and that means `<path>` with multiple subpaths. You can build it in either of two ways:

- **`evenodd`**: draw the inner outline in any direction. Its region is enclosed twice, which is an even count, so it's empty.
- **`nonzero`**: draw the inner outline in the **opposite direction** to the outer one. Its region's winding number is +1 −1 = 0, so it's empty.

<SvgSnippet>
{`
  <svg viewBox="0 0 220 110" width="440" height="220">
    <g>
      <path d="M10,10 H90 V90 H10 Z M30,30 H70 V70 H30 Z" fill="#0ea5e9" fill-opacity="0.5" stroke="#0369a1" stroke-width="2" fill-rule="evenodd" />
      <text x="50" y="105" font-size="8" text-anchor="middle" fill="currentColor">evenodd, same direction</text>
    </g>
    <g transform="translate(120,0)">
      <path d="M10,10 H90 V90 H10 Z M30,30 V70 H70 V30 Z" fill="#0ea5e9" fill-opacity="0.5" stroke="#0369a1" stroke-width="2" fill-rule="nonzero" />
      <text x="50" y="105" font-size="8" text-anchor="middle" fill="currentColor">nonzero, inner reversed</text>
    </g>
  </svg>
`}
</SvgSnippet>

<Info title="The keyhole hack">
  You *can* fake a hole in a single polygon by cutting a zero-width "slit" from the outer edge to the inner one and tracing both outlines as one loop. It works, but anti-aliasing often leaves a faint hairline along the slit, and the stroke draws the slit too. Use a `<path>` instead. [Compound paths and `fill-rule`](/post/svg/svg-essentials-mastering-the-path-element#compound-paths-and-the-fill-rule-property) are covered in depth in the path post.
</Info>

### Why This Matters

- **Stars and overlapping icons**: decide up front whether the doubly-enclosed centre should be solid (`nonzero`) or knocked out (`evenodd`), instead of leaving it to the default.
- **Geometric patterns**: with `evenodd`, regions where a self-intersecting outline overlaps itself alternate between filled and empty.
- **Data viz**: an area polygon whose data line crosses back over itself can create doubly-covered regions. Check which rule gives the result you intend.

**Summary:** `nonzero` fills every region the outline winds around, so self-overlaps stay solid. `evenodd` drops the regions covered an even number of times and keeps everything else. It doesn't turn a shape into a wireframe; it only changes the overlaps.

## Corners and the Miter Limit

Polygons and polylines are made entirely of straight segments, so their corners (the joins) and their loose ends (the caps) have an outsized effect on how polished they look. The basics are covered in [the basic shapes post](/post/svg/svg-essentials-mastering-basic-shapes#refining-the-stroke-look). In short:

- **`stroke-linejoin`** shapes every corner: `miter` (the default) gives sharp points, `round` gives rounded ones and `bevel` cuts them off flat.
- **`stroke-linecap`** shapes loose ends: `butt` (the default), `round` or `square`. On a polygon, it only matters when the stroke is dashed, since each dash then gets its own caps.

The attribute that surprises people is the third one.

### `stroke-miterlimit` in Depth

A miter join extends the two outer edges of the stroke until they meet. The sharper the corner, the farther away they meet. At a nearly-closed corner the spike would shoot out an absurd distance, so the renderer measures it first:

- The **miter length** is the distance from the inner corner of the join to the outer tip.
- For a corner of angle θ, the ratio **miter length ÷ stroke-width = 1 / sin(θ / 2)**.
- `stroke-miterlimit` sets the maximum allowed ratio. It **defaults to 4**, and values below 1 are invalid.
- If a corner's ratio is over the limit, that one join is drawn as a **bevel** instead. The other corners keep their miters.

With the default of 4, the cutoff is at θ = 2 · asin(1/4) ≈ **29°**. Any corner sharper than about 29° gets bevelled:

<MiterLimitDiagram />

That's why a star can come out looking "blunt" for no obvious reason. The regular five-pointed star from earlier has 36° tips (ratio ≈ 3.24), so its miters survive. Make the arms a little skinnier and the tips drop below 29°:

<SvgSnippet>
{`
  <svg viewBox="0 0 220 110" width="440" height="220">
    <g>
      <polygon points="50,8 57.1,40.3 89.9,37 61.4,53.7 74.7,84 50,62 25.3,84 38.6,53.7 10.1,37 42.9,40.3" fill="#f59e0b" fill-opacity="0.25" stroke="#b45309" stroke-width="5" stroke-linejoin="miter" />
      <text x="50" y="104" font-size="8" text-anchor="middle" fill="currentColor">miterlimit 4 (default)</text>
    </g>
    <g transform="translate(120,0)">
      <polygon points="50,8 57.1,40.3 89.9,37 61.4,53.7 74.7,84 50,62 25.3,84 38.6,53.7 10.1,37 42.9,40.3" fill="#f59e0b" fill-opacity="0.25" stroke="#b45309" stroke-width="5" stroke-linejoin="miter" stroke-miterlimit="5" />
      <text x="50" y="104" font-size="8" text-anchor="middle" fill="currentColor">miterlimit 5</text>
    </g>
  </svg>
`}
</SvgSnippet>

Each tip of this star is about 24.6°, which gives a ratio of about 4.69. With the default limit of 4 every tip is bevelled. Raising the limit to 5 lets the spikes through. The inner, concave corners are wide, so they're never affected.

Try it below. The corner starts at 25°, already past the limit, so the readout says bevel. Widen it past 29° to get the point back, then narrow it again and raise `stroke-miterlimit` instead. Stroke width changes how big the spike is but not the ratio, which depends only on the angle:

<Warning title="Raising the limit has a cost">
  A high `stroke-miterlimit` on a very sharp corner can produce spikes that poke well outside the shape's bounding box. They can get clipped by the `viewBox`, or they can overlap nearby content. If a shape's corners can get arbitrarily sharp, as with data-driven polylines, `stroke-linejoin="round"` is the safer choice. SVG 2 also defines `miter-clip` and `arcs` join values, but major browsers don't support them yet, so don't rely on either.
</Warning>

## Generating Points with JavaScript

Hand-writing coordinates works for a triangle. For anything regular, or anything data-driven, generate them.

### Regular Polygons and Stars

Every vertex of a regular polygon sits on a circle, so it's just `cos` and `sin` at evenly spaced angles. A star is the same idea with twice as many vertices, alternating between an outer and an inner radius:

<CodeBlock language="js">
{`
  // Returns a points string for a regular polygon (innerRadius omitted)
  // or a star (innerRadius given) centred on cx, cy.
  function polygonPoints(cx, cy, sides, radius, innerRadius) {
    const count = innerRadius ? sides * 2 : sides;
    const start = -Math.PI / 2; // first vertex at 12 o'clock (SVG's y-axis points down)

    return Array.from({ length: count }, (_, i) => {
      const r = innerRadius && i % 2 === 1 ? innerRadius : radius;
      const angle = start + (i * 2 * Math.PI) / count;
      const x = cx + r * Math.cos(angle);
      const y = cy + r * Math.sin(angle);
      return x.toFixed(1) + "," + y.toFixed(1);
    }).join(" ");
  }

  polygonPoints(50, 50, 6, 40);      // hexagon
  polygonPoints(50, 50, 5, 42, 18);  // five-pointed star
`}
</CodeBlock>

Two details are worth copying:

- **Start at −90°.** In SVG, angle 0 points right and angles increase **clockwise**, because y grows downward. Starting at −π/2 puts the first vertex at the top, so the shape stands upright.
- **Round with `toFixed()`.** Floating-point output like `84.64101615137754` makes the markup longer without looking any different. One or two decimal places is plenty for a typical `viewBox`.

Note that this star is drawn as its **outline** (10 vertices around the edge), not as a self-intersecting pentagram. It has no doubly-covered centre, so it looks the same under either fill rule.

### Mapping Data to Points

For a chart, each value needs to be mapped from data space into the `viewBox`. That takes a linear scale on each axis, and the y-axis needs flipping because SVG's y grows downward:

<CodeBlock language="js">
{`
  // Maps an array of numbers onto a width × height viewBox, leaving pad units of breathing room.
  function toPoints(data, width, height, pad = 2) {
    const min = Math.min(...data);
    const max = Math.max(...data);
    const xStep = (width - pad * 2) / (data.length - 1);
    const yScale = (height - pad * 2) / (max - min || 1); // || 1 guards a flat series

    return data
      .map((value, i) => {
        const x = pad + i * xStep;
        const y = height - pad - (value - min) * yScale; // flip: larger values sit higher
        return x.toFixed(1) + "," + y.toFixed(1);
      })
      .join(" ");
  }

  document.querySelector("#trend").setAttribute("points", toPoints([12, 15, 11, 19, 17, 24, 22, 27], 100, 30));
`}
</CodeBlock>

Charting libraries like D3 wrap exactly this in `scaleLinear()`, but for a single line, a dozen lines of code is all you need.

### The Live `points` List: `SVGPointList`

You don't have to rebuild the whole string to change one vertex. Every `<polyline>` and `<polygon>` exposes its vertices as a **live** `points` list, and changing an item redraws the shape:

<CodeBlock language="js">
{`
  const line = document.querySelector("#trend");

  line.points.numberOfItems;          // how many vertices
  const first = line.points.getItem(0);
  first.y = 5;                        // moves the first vertex, redraws immediately

  // Append a new vertex
  const svg = line.ownerSVGElement;
  const point = svg.createSVGPoint();
  point.x = 98;
  point.y = 4;
  line.points.appendItem(point);
`}
</CodeBlock>

`insertItemBefore()`, `replaceItem()`, `removeItem()` and `clear()` round out the list API. `createSVGPoint()` is the most widely supported way to make a point object. SVG 2 lets list items be plain `DOMPoint`s, but test that in your target browsers before depending on it. When the *whole* dataset changes, setting the `points` attribute once is simpler, and usually cheaper, than dozens of individual list mutations.

## Sparklines: Tiny Charts That Stretch

A sparkline is a word-sized line chart that sits inline with text or in a table cell, with no axes and no labels. It's the most natural job there is for `<polyline>`, and it shows off three attributes working together:

<SvgSnippet>
{`
  <svg viewBox="0 0 100 30" width="240" height="48" preserveAspectRatio="none" role="img" aria-labelledby="sparkline-title">
    <title id="sparkline-title">Weekly sign-ups, rising from 12 to 27 over eight weeks</title>
    <polyline points="2,26.4 15.7,21.5 29.4,28 43.1,15 56.9,18.3 70.6,6.9 84.3,10.1 98,2" fill="none" stroke="#6366f1" stroke-width="2" stroke-linejoin="round" stroke-linecap="round" vector-effect="non-scaling-stroke" />
    <line x1="98" y1="2" x2="98" y2="2" stroke="#6366f1" stroke-width="6" stroke-linecap="round" vector-effect="non-scaling-stroke" />
  </svg>
`}
</SvgSnippet>

- **`viewBox="0 0 100 30"`** gives the data a fixed coordinate space. These points came straight from the `toPoints()` function above.
- **`preserveAspectRatio="none"`** lets that space stretch to whatever box the sparkline is given, whether a narrow table cell or a full-width card, without letterboxing. The line is squashed or stretched, which is fine, because a sparkline shows shape, not exact values.
- **`vector-effect="non-scaling-stroke"`** fixes the side effect. Without it, a non-uniform stretch distorts the stroke too, so the line gets thick on steep sections and thin on flat ones. With it, the stroke width is applied in screen pixels after the stretch, so the line is a consistent 2px everywhere. This is a **visual** fix, not a performance one.

The last-point dot uses a small trick: it's a **zero-length `<line>` with a round cap**, which renders as a dot the width of the stroke. A `<circle>` would be squashed into an ellipse by the non-uniform stretch, but the cap is also `non-scaling-stroke`, so it stays round. If you'd rather not rely on that, position an HTML dot over the SVG instead.

<Info title="Where the stroke width comes from">
  With `non-scaling-stroke`, `stroke-width="2"` means 2 CSS pixels on screen, whatever the SVG's size. That's usually what you want for charts, where lines should stay the same weight as the chart grows. The same trick is useful anywhere a line-art icon or map is displayed at very different sizes.
</Info>

## Markers on Vertices

Charts often need a dot on every data point or an arrowhead at the end of a route. You don't need to compute those positions: `<polyline>` and `<polygon>` both support `marker-start`, `marker-mid` and `marker-end`, and the browser places a marker on each vertex for you:

<SvgSnippet>
{`
  <svg viewBox="0 0 120 70" width="360" height="210">
    <defs>
      <marker id="pvp-marker-dot" viewBox="0 0 10 10" refX="5" refY="5" markerWidth="4" markerHeight="4">
        <circle cx="5" cy="5" r="4" fill="#6366f1" />
      </marker>
      <marker id="pvp-marker-arrow" viewBox="0 0 10 10" refX="8" refY="5" markerWidth="5" markerHeight="5" orient="auto">
        <path d="M0,0 L10,5 L0,10 Z" fill="#6366f1" />
      </marker>
    </defs>
    <polyline points="10,55 35,30 60,40 85,15 105,20" fill="none" stroke="#6366f1" stroke-width="2" stroke-linejoin="round" marker-start="url(#pvp-marker-dot)" marker-mid="url(#pvp-marker-dot)" marker-end="url(#pvp-marker-arrow)" />
  </svg>
`}
</SvgSnippet>

`marker-mid` lands on every interior vertex, so adding a data point to the `points` list adds its dot automatically.

## Draw-On Animation

The "line drawing itself" effect works on `<polyline>` and `<polygon>` exactly as it does on `<path>`. Set `pathLength="1"` so the shape's length is normalised to 1. Then use a dash as long as the whole shape, and slide it into view with `stroke-dashoffset`:

<CodeBlock language="xml">
{`
  <svg viewBox="0 0 100 60">
    <style>
      .pvp-draw {
        stroke-dasharray: 1;
        stroke-dashoffset: 1;
        animation: pvp-draw 2s ease-out forwards;
      }

      @keyframes pvp-draw {
        to { stroke-dashoffset: 0; }
      }

      @media (prefers-reduced-motion: reduce) {
        .pvp-draw { animation: none; stroke-dashoffset: 0; }
      }
    </style>

    <polyline class="pvp-draw" pathLength="1" points="5,50 25,20 45,35 65,10 95,25" fill="none" stroke="#10b981" stroke-width="3" stroke-linejoin="round" />
  </svg>
`}
</CodeBlock>

A few things to keep in mind:

- On a **polygon**, the closing segment is part of the length, so the outline draws all the way back to its start.
- `pathLength` is defined for all basic shapes in SVG 2. If an engine you support ignores it on a polyline, measure the real length with `element.getTotalLength()` and use that number for the dasharray and offset instead of `1`.
- A shape that's still filled will show its full fill from the first frame. Draw-on is a stroke effect, so either use `fill="none"` or fade the fill in separately.

The [path element post](/post/svg/svg-essentials-mastering-the-path-element) walks through the dash maths step by step, and [Animating SVG: A Guide to CSS Transitions and Keyframes](/post/animations/svg-transitions-and-keyframes) covers triggering it on hover or scroll.

## Morphing Shapes: Animating `points`

Unlike `<path>`'s `d`, which some browsers expose as a CSS property, **`points` is not a CSS property**. You can't put it in a `transition` or `@keyframes` rule. You have three options:

- **SMIL** `<animate attributeName="points">`, declared right inside the shape.
- **JavaScript**: interpolate each coordinate yourself in a `requestAnimationFrame` loop and write the result back.
- **An animation library** (GSAP, anime.js and similar), which handles the interpolation for you.

All three interpolate point by point, so both shapes need **the same number of points**. If the counts differ, the browser can't pair the vertices up, and SMIL falls back to a discrete jump from one shape to the other with no tween. To morph a triangle into a square, pad the triangle to four points by repeating its top vertex, so the apex splits into the square's two top corners:

<CodeBlock language="xml">
{`
  <svg viewBox="0 0 100 100">
    <polygon points="50,10 50,10 90,90 10,90" fill="#f59e0b" fill-opacity="0.5" stroke="#b45309" stroke-width="2">
      <animate
        attributeName="points"
        dur="2s"
        repeatCount="indefinite"
        values="50,10 50,10 90,90 10,90;
                10,10 90,10 90,90 10,90;
                50,10 50,10 90,90 10,90" />
    </polygon>
  </svg>
`}
</CodeBlock>

Where you put the duplicate matters. Each vertex travels in a straight line to its partner, so match vertices that are close together, or the shape will twist through itself mid-morph.

## Putting it into Practice

These examples show how to choose the right element for common jobs.

### Basic: Geometric Essentials

#### 1. The Simplest Triangle (`<polygon>`)

The most fundamental polygon is a triangle. With just three points, SVG connects the last vertex back to the first and encloses the shape.

<SvgSnippet>
{`
  <svg viewBox="0 0 100 100" width="220" height="220">
    <polygon points="50,10 90,85 10,85" fill="#38bdf8" fill-opacity="0.25" stroke="#0369a1" stroke-width="3" stroke-linejoin="round" />
  </svg>
`}
</SvgSnippet>

**Why it works:** `stroke-linejoin="round"` softens the corners, which gives the shape a more modern, friendly look. It also sidesteps the miter limit entirely.

#### 2. Route Map Visualization (`<polyline>`)

A route shouldn't close back on itself, which makes it a natural polyline. Here it's drawn as a dashed "connect-the-dots" path.

<SvgSnippet>
{`
  <svg viewBox="0 0 100 100" width="240" height="240">
    <polyline points="10,85 30,55 50,70 70,25 90,15" fill="none" stroke="#10b981" stroke-width="3" stroke-linecap="round" stroke-linejoin="round" stroke-dasharray="6 4" />
    <circle cx="10" cy="85" r="3" fill="#10b981" />
    <circle cx="90" cy="15" r="3" fill="#10b981" />
  </svg>
`}
</SvgSnippet>

**Pro Tip:** `fill="none"` avoids the phantom fill, and `stroke-dasharray` gives the dashed look common in map apps. The start and end circles could just as well be `marker-start` and `marker-end`.

### Advanced: Data & UI

#### 1. The Star Shape (`evenodd` Rule)

When a polygon crosses itself, as this pentagram does, `fill-rule` decides whether the doubly-enclosed centre is hollow or solid.

<SvgSnippet>
{`
  <svg viewBox="0 0 100 100" width="240" height="240">
    <polygon points="50,5 76.4,86.4 7.2,36.1 92.8,36.1 23.5,86.4" fill="#8b5cf6" fill-opacity="0.6" stroke="#6d28d9" stroke-width="2" fill-rule="evenodd" />
  </svg>
`}
</SvgSnippet>

**Why it matters:** `evenodd` knocks out the self-overlapping centre with a single element. It works because the outline crosses itself. It can't add a separate hole to a shape that doesn't overlap itself; for that, see [holes](#what-a-single-polygon-cant-do-holes).

#### 2. The Hybrid Area Chart

Data visualization often combines both elements. A `polyline` draws the trend line, and a `polygon` shades the area underneath. Here the area fades out using a vertical gradient:

<SvgSnippet>
{`
  <svg viewBox="0 0 100 100" width="280" height="180">
    <defs>
      <linearGradient id="pvp-area-fade" x1="0" y1="0" x2="0" y2="1">
        <stop offset="0" stop-color="#6366f1" stop-opacity="0.35" />
        <stop offset="1" stop-color="#6366f1" stop-opacity="0" />
      </linearGradient>
    </defs>
    <polygon points="0,100 0,70 20,50 40,60 60,30 80,45 100,20 100,100" fill="url(#pvp-area-fade)" stroke="none" />
    <polyline points="0,70 20,50 40,60 60,30 80,45 100,20" fill="none" stroke="#6366f1" stroke-width="2.5" stroke-linecap="round" stroke-linejoin="round" />
  </svg>
`}
</SvgSnippet>

**Strategy:** For the polygon to sit flat against the bottom, include the "floor" coordinates (`0,100` and `100,100`) in its `points`. Keep the stroke on a separate polyline. Otherwise, the polygon would also stroke its two vertical sides and the floor.

#### 3. Geometric UI Hexagon

Polygons are the go-to for rigid UI elements like hex-grid buttons or achievement badges.

<SvgSnippet>
{`
  <svg viewBox="0 0 100 100" width="220" height="220">
    <polygon points="50,10 84.6,30 84.6,70 50,90 15.4,70 15.4,30" fill="#f43f5e" fill-opacity="0.2" stroke="#be123c" stroke-width="3" stroke-linejoin="round" />
  </svg>
`}
</SvgSnippet>

**Context:** A handful of bytes of markup replaces an image file, and the shape stays crisp at any pixel density or zoom level. Set **Preset** to `hexagon` in the [Shape Presets playground](#interactive-example) above to try it, or generate one with `polygonPoints(50, 50, 6, 40)`.

## When to Switch to `<path>`

`<polyline>` and `<polygon>` are shorthands for one specific kind of `<path>`: a single subpath of straight lines. Reach for [`<path>`](/post/svg/svg-essentials-mastering-the-path-element) as soon as you need any of these:

- **Curves**: Bézier curves (`C`, `S`, `Q`, `T`), for smooth chart lines, organic shapes or rounded corners on only some vertices.
- **Arcs** (`A`), for pie slices, gauges and rounded ends of a specific radius.
- **Holes or multiple outlines**: several subpaths in one element, as in the square frame above.
- **Many disjoint lines in one element**: grid lines, tick marks or a set of separate routes as one DOM node, each starting with its own `M`.

Converting is mechanical. A polyline's points become an `M` followed by `L` commands, and a polygon adds a closing `Z`:

<CodeBlock language="xml">
{`
  <polyline points="10,80 40,20 70,60" />
  <path d="M10,80 L40,20 L70,60" />

  <polygon points="10,80 40,20 70,60" />
  <path d="M10,80 L40,20 L70,60 Z" />
`}
</CodeBlock>

The `Z` is what gives you the polygon's clean closing join. Ending a path with `L10,80` instead would recreate the [repeated-point seam](#explicit-closing-a-repeated-point-isnt-a-polygon). Going the other way, if a path is a single `M` followed only by `L`, `H`, `V` and at most one closing `Z`, a polyline or polygon is usually easier to read and to generate.

<RainbowDivider />

## Performance: Point Density

A few dozen or a few hundred points cost nothing. Trouble starts with thousands of points, such as a high-resolution map outline or an "all-time" stock chart, especially when they're updated often. What actually helps:

- **Draw fewer points.** A 600px-wide chart can't show more than a point or two per pixel column, so 10,000 raw samples are mostly invisible. Simplify the line first with the **Ramer–Douglas–Peucker** algorithm (for example, the [simplify.js](https://mourner.github.io/simplify-js/) library), or downsample to one min/max pair per pixel. This is by far the biggest win.
- **Round your coordinates.** `toFixed(1)` or `toFixed(2)` shrinks the markup without any visible change.
- **Transform instead of recomputing.** To pan or zoom a chart, put a `transform` on a parent `<g>` or change the `viewBox`, rather than rewriting every coordinate on every frame.
- **Don't reset `points` every frame** unless the data really changed. Batch updates, and for a single moving vertex, use the live `points` list.
- **Know when to leave SVG.** For tens of thousands of points, or for real-time streams redrawn many times a second, `<canvas>` is usually the better tool, because it paints pixels without keeping a DOM node per shape.

<Info title="What SVGO does and doesn't do">
  SVGO and similar optimizers shrink the **markup**: they round numeric precision, strip whitespace and metadata, and can convert shapes to `<path>`. They don't reduce the **number of vertices** in a line. For that, run a simplification step (RDP / simplify.js) on the data before it becomes markup.
</Info>

`vector-effect="non-scaling-stroke"` often shows up in performance advice, but it's a visual fix that keeps stroke width consistent under scaling. See [Sparklines](#sparklines-tiny-charts-that-stretch) for where it belongs.

## Accessibility Considerations

`<polygon>` and `<polyline>` are ordinary DOM elements, so the general guidance from [SVG Essentials: Mastering Shapes, Coordinates, and Styling](/post/svg/svg-essentials-mastering-basic-shapes#making-svgs-accessible) applies. Two patterns come up often enough with point-based shapes to call out on their own.

**Icons and badges built from `<polygon>`:** if the shape conveys meaning on its own (a hexagon "verified" badge, a star rating), give it a `<title>` and `role="img"`. If it's purely decorative and sits next to a text label that already says the same thing, hide it with `aria-hidden="true"`.

**Charts built from `<polyline>`:** a `points` string is just coordinates, so there's nothing in it a screen reader can announce. Give the chart a short name and a one-sentence summary of the trend, and make the exact values available as a real table:

<CodeBlock language="html">
{`
  <figure>
    <svg viewBox="0 0 100 30" role="img" aria-labelledby="signups-title signups-desc">
      <title id="signups-title">Weekly sign-ups, weeks 1 to 8</title>
      <desc id="signups-desc">Sign-ups more than doubled, from 12 in week 1 to 27 in week 8, with small dips in weeks 3, 5 and 7.</desc>
      <polyline points="2,26.4 15.7,21.5 29.4,28 43.1,15 56.9,18.3 70.6,6.9 84.3,10.1 98,2"
                fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2" />
    </svg>

    <details>
      <summary>Show the data</summary>
      <table>
        <thead><tr><th scope="col">Week</th><th scope="col">Sign-ups</th></tr></thead>
        <tbody>
          <tr><td>1</td><td>12</td></tr>
          <tr><td>2</td><td>15</td></tr>
          <!-- … weeks 3–8 … -->
        </tbody>
      </table>
    </details>
  </figure>
`}
</CodeBlock>

- `role="img"` plus `aria-labelledby` pointing at both the `<title>` and `<desc>` gives the graphic a name and a description. It also tells assistive tech to treat the SVG as one image, instead of walking its children.
- The `<desc>` states the **takeaway**, not the coordinates. That's what a sighted reader gets from a glance.
- The table carries the exact values for anyone who needs them, and it's useful to sighted users too. A visually-hidden table works as well, if you don't want a disclosure widget.

<Warning title="Don't rely on color or shape alone">
  If a chart uses `fill-rule` or overlapping shapes to distinguish data series, make sure that distinction doesn't depend on color alone. Pair it with labels, patterns or a legend so the information still reads for colorblind users.
</Warning>

## Best Practices

1. **Always define `fill` for polylines.** The default is `fill="black"`, so set `fill="none"` explicitly to avoid accidental shading.
2. **Close with `<polygon>`, not a repeated point.** A repeated first point leaves a capped seam instead of a join.
3. **Order points deliberately.** They're connected exactly in the order written, so sort generated points by angle or by x.
4. **Watch sharp corners.** Corners under about 29° are bevelled by the default `stroke-miterlimit="4"`. Raise the limit on purpose, or use `stroke-linejoin="round"` for data-driven shapes.
5. **Choose your `fill-rule` deliberately.** For self-intersecting shapes, decide up front between a solid (`nonzero`) and a knocked-out (`evenodd`) centre. For real holes, switch to `<path>`.
6. **Round generated coordinates** with `toFixed()`, and simplify dense data before rendering it.
7. **Use a consistent coordinate grid.** A predictable grid like `viewBox="0 0 100 100"` makes positioning and generating points easier.
8. **Give meaningful shapes a name.** Icons and badges need a `<title>` (or `aria-hidden="true"` if decorative), and charts need a summary plus the data.

## Where Should You Use This?

**Use `<polyline>` when:**

- Building line charts and sparklines
- Drawing routes, connectors or signal traces
- Connecting data points, with markers on the vertices
- Animating a line drawing itself in

**Use `<polygon>` when:**

- Creating geometric shapes, stars, hexagons and badges
- Filling closed areas, like the area under a chart or map regions
- Designing rigid UI illustrations
- Morphing between shapes with matching point counts

**Use `<path>` when** you need curves, arcs, holes or several outlines in one element. See [When to Switch to `<path>`](#when-to-switch-to-path).

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Can a `<polyline>` be filled?

Yes, and by default it will be, whether you want it to or not. SVG's initial `fill` value is black, and a polyline is no exception, even though it's an "open" shape. The fill covers the region enclosed by the line plus an imaginary chord from the last point back to the first. Add `fill="none"` unless you want that region shaded.

---

### Does `<polygon>` support `stroke-dasharray`?

Yes. A dashed polygon still closes itself, and the dash pattern runs continuously around the whole outline, including the auto-generated closing segment. Each dash gets its own `stroke-linecap`.

---

### Can I animate the `points` attribute?

Yes, but not with CSS. `points` isn't a CSS property. Use SMIL's `<animate attributeName="points">`, JavaScript or an animation library. Smooth interpolation only happens between lists with the **same number of points**. Mismatched counts jump from one shape to the other instead. See [Morphing Shapes](#morphing-shapes-animating-points).

---

### Why does `fill-rule` change the filled area but not the outline?

Because `fill-rule` only governs the **fill**. It decides which enclosed regions count as "inside". The stroke follows the same edges whichever rule is active, so the outline never changes; only the shaded area does.

---

### Why are the tips of my star blunt?

The tips are sharper than about 29°, so they exceed the default `stroke-miterlimit` of 4 and fall back to bevels. Raise `stroke-miterlimit`, or accept rounded tips with `stroke-linejoin="round"`. See [the miter limit](#stroke-miterlimit-in-depth).

---

### Can a `<polygon>` or `<polyline>` have curved segments?

No. Both elements only connect their points with straight lines. For Bézier curves and arcs, use [`<path>`](/post/svg/svg-essentials-mastering-the-path-element).

## Wrapping Up

`<polygon>` and `<polyline>` share the exact same `points` syntax, and most of the time the choice comes down to one question: does this shape need to close?

- **Use `polyline`** for data visualizations, sparklines, route maps and anything that should stay open. Remember `fill="none"`.
- **Use `polygon`** for icons, badges and geometric shapes that need a sealed, cleanly-joined outline. Don't fake one by repeating the first point.
- **Watch your `fill-rule`** whenever an outline crosses itself. `nonzero` counts direction and `evenodd` counts crossings, and they only disagree about regions wound an even, non-zero number of times.
- **Mind the miter limit** on sharp corners: past about 29°, the default of 4 turns spikes into bevels.
- **Generate, don't hand-type**: a little `cos`/`sin` or a linear scale produces precise, rounded `points` for stars, polygons and charts.
- **Graduate to `<path>`** for curves, arcs and holes.

Both elements trace back to the six basic shapes covered in
[SVG Essentials: Mastering Shapes, Coordinates, and Styling](/post/svg/svg-essentials-mastering-basic-shapes), which is worth a revisit if the
`points` syntax or coordinate system ever feels shaky. From here, [SVG Essentials: Mastering the Path Element](/post/svg/svg-essentials-mastering-the-path-element) picks up where straight lines leave off.
